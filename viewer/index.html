        Help

        Blink images taken years apart. Stars hold still; nearby or distant movers do not.

        Find a mover

        1. Center a field. Type right ascension and declination in decimal degrees and press Enter, or double-click the sky to recenter on that spot.
        2. Blink the epochs. Press play and watch the crosshair area. Widen the sliding window to beat down noise, or slow the blink interval to follow faint sources.
        3. Check what moved. Turn on the Gaia DR3 overlay: a new mover usually has no circle. Difference imaging turns it into a bright and dark pair, and Shift and add collapses it to a point when the motion matches.

        Try it on the default field: the test object PX-1 drifts past the crosshair. Open Shift and add and choose “Use PX-1 motion”.

        What each control does

        Sliding window
        Averages all epochs inside this many years into one frame. Wider windows lower the noise but smear fast movers.
        Blink interval
        How long each frame stays on screen.
        Zoom
        Screen pixels per 2.75″ WISE pixel. SPHEREx’s larger 6.15″ pixels keep the same angular scale.
        Min - max pixel values
        The clip range applied before stretching.
        Stretch Linearity
        1.000 is close to linear. Lower values lift faint sources (asinh stretch).
        Invert pixel values
        Dark sources on a light sky, which makes faint movers easier to spot.
        Maximize dynamic range
        Sets the clip range for each frame from its 0.5 and 99.5 percentiles.
        Separate scan directions
        Splits each epoch into ascending and descending scans, so parallax shows as a hop back and forth.
        Use only NEOWISE imagery
        Drops the 2010–2011 WISE epochs.
        Skip middle epochs
        Blinks only the first and last frames, the longest time baseline.
        Skip duplicate windows
        Drops windows that add no epochs beyond the previous frame.
        Smooth by scan direction
        Light 3×3 smoothing that suppresses single-pixel noise.
        Border epoch 0
        Outlines the first frame so you can see where the loop starts.
        Gaia DR3 overlay
        On SPHEREx D1–D6: rings every Gaia DR3 star that SPHEREx actually sees in the current frame, in the star’s real colour (blue = hot, red = cool; see the legend), moved to the frame’s date by proper motion. Fast movers get a dashed arrow from their 2016 position.
        Star cards
        On SPHEREx D1–D6, hover a star for its identity card: Gaia name, constellation, history, distance, size, mass, temperature, colour, brightness, age, planets, special phenomena and a spectrum strip coloured by element. Click to pin it, Esc to close.
        Synthetic objects
        Inject test movers with a chosen motion and W2 magnitude.

        About

        The missions behind the imagery, and what this viewer is.

        SPHEREx

        The Spectro-Photometer for the History of the Universe, Epoch of Reionization and Ices Explorer is a NASA mission managed by JPL. It launched on 11 March 2025, began survey operations on 1 May 2025 and finished its first all-sky map in December 2025. Every six months it images the entire sky in 102 colors of infrared light; the two-year prime mission plans four all-sky maps, with data released through NASA/IPAC IRSA.

        WISE and NEOWISE

        WISE surveyed the sky from 2010; reactivated as NEOWISE, it kept imaging at 3.4 and 4.6 µm (W1 and W2) from December 2013 until August 2024. Those repeated passes over more than a decade are what make blink searches for cold, nearby brown dwarfs possible.

        Planet X

        “Planet X” is the long-standing name for an undiscovered planet beyond Neptune. Its modern form, Planet Nine, was proposed in 2016 by Konstantin Batygin and Mike Brown to explain the clustered orbits of some extreme trans-Neptunian objects. As of 2026 no such planet has been confirmed: candidates picked out of archival infrared surveys still await follow-up, and some newly found distant objects have orbits that argue against it.

        This viewer

        The SPHEREx D1–D6 bands show real data. When the page is served by serve.py, it asks NASA/IPAC IRSA (SIA v2, collections spherex_dr1 and spherex_qr3) for every Level-2 exposure covering the position, keeps a few per visit, downloads small cutouts, resamples each onto a common north-up 6.15″/px grid with its own TAN-SIP WCS and subtracts the background. Frames are then grouped by the time window, so the default shows one co-added frame per visit, about six months apart. SPHEREx uses linear variable filters, so a source is seen at a slightly different wavelength in each exposure, and some brightness change between frames is expected. Loaded fields are cached in spherex_cache/ and still work offline.

        The 14 years option shows real photographs too: single exposures from WISE (2010) and its NEOWISE reactivation (2014 to 2024) at 4.6 µm, from the IRSA image service, one per visit (about every six months), resampled onto the same north-up grid with each frame’s own WCS. WISE was switched off between 2011 and 2013, so there are no photographs for those years, and the time bar says so. Very bright stars overflow the detector; those pixels are flagged by NASA, hatched on screen and shown at the brightest value on their rim, which is a lower limit. Barnard’s star crosses about 145″ between 2010 and 2024. Alignment was checked against Gaia DR3: positions agree to within about one pixel in every frame. Older simulated bands (made from Gaia stars for testing) are hidden from the menu and are always labelled “Simulated”; nothing on the default screens is simulated.
